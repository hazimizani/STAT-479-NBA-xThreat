Project 1: Deriving Expected Possession Values and ‘Threat’ from Play-By-Play Context

Author

Rohan Ahuja, Hazim bin Mohd Izani, Mason Langer

Executive Summary

Our project aims to define the expected outcome value of an in-game possession based on sequences of play through publicly available NBA play-by-play data. Specifically, the model outlined in the following report intends to show the expected points from a given possession based on key actions, including how the sequence began (rebound, turnover), passing players, and the location that the sequence ended on the court. From this data and the our resulting models, we’ve highlighted multiple actions that optimize the expected outcome of a sequence and what players exceeded their expected value based on the plays they were on the court for.

Similar advanced statistics have been proposed and implemented for basketball analytics. Particularly the likes of Adjusted Plus-Minus (+/-) have long been used to estimate the efficacy of a player based on their team’s performance while on the court. Further, football (soccer) analytics utilizes an expected goals (xG) model to estimate the probability that a shot results in a goal given a number of factors, including where the shot occured on the pitch, where the nearest defender was, and whether the player was on the left or the right side of the field. These statistics provided motivation for our model, which considered aspects of both, namely player contribution and expectation based on play context. However, our derived metric diverged, aiming to abstract higher than the single player action-level (e.g. a shot) while also identifying players who initiated/ended successful sequences of play at a higher rate than their peers.

In order to engineer an accurate final expected possession value model, we iteratively developed a workflow of three different models by using a set of play-by-play data to conclude a successful estimated threat model. With the final model in place and subsequent analysis of its results based on statistical insights and qualitative analysis, we have found that the expected threat (xT) metric is able to holistically model a player’s impact on the court. Particularly, using the metric as either a total (a player’s impact over a full season) or as an average per sequence of play, two different insights can be learned. For the former, xT tends to converge with other advanced metrics often employed to describe elite, All-Star athletes or MVP-caliber players. For instance, with this approach, we have found players like former MVPs such as Giannis Antetokounmpo and Nikola Jokic, alongside burgeoning star players like Alperen Sengun and Anthony Edwards, to be among the upper echelon of total xT. On the other hand, viewing the metric as an average per sequence of play tends to reveal players that may not traditionally be among the highest caliber of player, but take a high volume of highly advantageous shots, like Malik Beasley, OG Anunoby, and Darius Garland.

Gathering Data

Data for the report was sourced using the hoopR library to gather play-by-play (often denoted as pbp) data and detailed shot-related data. With this, we were able to derive relative coordinates where plays were initiated and ended, alongside distance to the offensive team’s basket, and how a sequence of play started.

Specific hoopR endpoints that were used throughout our research include load_nba_pbp and nba_shotchartdetail, along with scripts wrote to parse PBP data into condensed features representing a “sequence of play.” These sequences of play, as detailed above, include coordinates for the starting and ending locations, the action that started a play and ended the sequence, distance to hoop, and which area of the court the play ended.

to01 <- function(y) {
  if (is.factor(y)) as.numeric(y) - 1L else as.numeric(y)

brier <- function(y, p) {
  y_num <- to01(y)
  p <- pmin(pmax(p, 1e-12), 1 - 1e-12)
  mean((y_num - p)^2, na.rm = TRUE)
}

logloss <- function(y, p) {
  y_num <- to01(y)
  p <- pmin(pmax(p, 1e-12), 1 - 1e-12)
  -mean(y_num*log(p) + (1 - y_num)*log(1 - p), na.rm = TRUE)
}

eval_model <- function(name, y_tr, p_tr, y_te, p_te){

      "| LogLoss:", round(logloss(y_te, p_te), 4))
}

safe_coalesce_chr <- function(df, candidates) {
  v <- rep(NA_character_, nrow(df))
  for (nm in candidates) {
    if (nm %in% names(df)) {
      src <- df[[nm]]
      csrc <- as.character(src)
      csrc[is.na(src)] <- NA_character_

  }
  v

Deriving XPts with Laplace Smoothing and Random Forest Approaches

In order to estimate the value of a shot given its location, two approaches were identified: a Bayesian conditional probability approach leveraging binning based on shot location with Laplace smoothing, and a Random Forest classifer.

With the Laplace Smoothing approach, we can determine a foundation for areas of the court that optimize a shot’s value. Rather intuitively, this tends to skew toward shots closer to the basket, while some grids close to half-court were identified as having a high EP/FGA. These cases are likely due to a relative small amount of shot attempts within those bins, with a relatively high amount of successful events.

This approach can be used to underlie the intuition of a regression task to utilize detailed shot information such as coordinate placement, the angle to the basket, and indicators for shot-related attributes to estimate the probability of a successful shot. Given the high-dimensional aspect of this dataset, this regression task was modeled using a Random Forest.

norm_xy <- function(v, rng) (v - rng[1]) / (rng[2] - rng[1])

# If your court is flipped vertically relative to the offensive hoop, set FLIP_Y <- TRUE
FLIP_Y <- FALSE

shots <- shots %>%

The following approach utilizes inspiration from the two described approaches to identifying EPV of a sequence based on the initiating action, along with determining the expected points of a shot given location data.

Unlike the EPV approach above, this EPV approach leverages access to X and Y coordinate data to perform logistic regression with glm on a success attribute, which identifies if a sequence of play ended in a successful shot. Further, the starting (initiating) actions defined in the dataset diverge slightly from the previously identified start contexts. Primarily, this dataset now includes actions like steals and violations, alongside the previously defined rebounds and inbound plays.

Conclusion

Based on the findings above, we can see that there is merit in utilizing an xT-related metric for multifaceted use cases. Primarily, given the subsequent analysis above when applying our models to the 2024 NBA regular season, our findings have indicated that the combination of initial EPV and xPts based on ending location, given play-by-play data, can be applied to define highly impactful players over the entirety of an NBA season, or to define highly efficient players given their shot selection when viewing xT per sequence. Further, the combination of both EPV and xPts, rather than leveraging one or the other, provides a considerable benefit when assessing a player holistically rather than focusing on “one end of the court.”

Even so, our modelling procedure and analysis can be further developed, primarily regarding the usage of different statistical applications. Specifically, within our initial research process, XGBoost was identified as a target to use either for xPts modelling, or deriving an estimated value for whole sequence. While the approach we’ve identified above evidently does indicate player value and impact, leveraging XGBoost would provide another lens to assessing the various subproblems.
